Add fractions

Add fractions using a common denominator.

Practice this skill →

The idea

Multiply the numerator and denominator by the same number to make equivalent fractions. Add the numerators; keep the common denominator.
  1. Find a common multiple of the denominators.
  2. Rewrite both fractions with that denominator.
  3. Add the numerators, then simplify if possible.

Example 1   1/3 + 1/4

  1. A common denominator is 12.
  2. 1/3 = 4/12 and 1/4 = 3/12.
  3. 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12.

Example 2   2/5 + 1/10

  1. Use a denominator of 10.
  2. 2/5 = 4/10.
  3. 4/10 + 1/10 = 5/10 = 1/2.
Watch for this

Do not add the denominators. They describe the size of each part, and that size must stay the same.

Percent of a number

Convert a percent to a decimal and find a part of a whole.

Practice this skill →

The idea

A percent is a number out of 100. To find p% of a number, multiply that number by p ÷ 100.
  1. Divide the percent by 100.
  2. Multiply the resulting decimal by the whole.
  3. Check that a percent below 100 gives a result smaller than the whole.

Example 1   Find 15% of 80.

  1. 15 ÷ 100 = 0.15.
  2. 0.15 × 80 = 12.

Example 2   Find 25% of 60.

  1. 25 ÷ 100 = 0.25.
  2. 0.25 × 60 = 15.
  3. You can also divide 60 by 4 because 25% is one quarter.
Watch for this

15% means 0.15, not 15. Forgetting to divide by 100 makes the answer one hundred times too large.

Equivalent ratios

Scale both parts of a ratio by the same factor.

Practice this skill →

The idea

Equivalent ratios compare amounts in the same proportion. Multiply or divide both quantities by the same nonzero number.
  1. Find the multiplier between the known quantities.
  2. Apply that multiplier to the other quantity.
  3. Check both pairs describe the same comparison.

Example 1   3 notebooks cost $6. What do 7 notebooks cost?

  1. One notebook costs 6 ÷ 3 = $2.
  2. Seven notebooks cost 7 × 2 = $14.

Example 2   A recipe uses 2 cups of rice for 3 cups of water. How much water for 6 cups of rice?

  1. 6 ÷ 2 = 3, so the recipe is tripled.
  2. 3 × 3 = 9 cups of water.
Watch for this

Adding the same amount to both sides of a ratio usually changes the ratio. Use multiplication or division.

One-step equations

Use an inverse operation to isolate the variable.

Practice this skill →

The idea

An equation is a balance. Adding or subtracting the same amount on both sides keeps it balanced.
  1. Identify what has been added to or subtracted from x.
  2. Undo that operation on both sides.
  3. Substitute your answer into the original equation.

Example 1   x + 7 = 19

  1. Subtract 7 from both sides.
  2. x = 19 − 7 = 12.
  3. Check: 12 + 7 = 19.

Example 2   x − 5 = 8

  1. Add 5 to both sides.
  2. x = 8 + 5 = 13.
Watch for this

Whatever you do to one side of the equation, do to the other side too.

Two-step equations

Solve equations in the form ax + b = c.

Practice this skill →

The idea

Undo addition or subtraction first, then undo multiplication or division. This isolates x while keeping both sides equal.
  1. Subtract the constant from both sides.
  2. Divide both sides by the coefficient of x.
  3. Substitute the result to check it.

Example 1   3x + 5 = 20

  1. Subtract 5: 3x = 15.
  2. Divide by 3: x = 5.
  3. Check: 3 × 5 + 5 = 20.

Example 2   4x + 2 = 30

  1. Subtract 2: 4x = 28.
  2. Divide by 4: x = 7.
Watch for this

Divide the entire remaining side by the coefficient, not just one term of the original equation.

Equations with parentheses

Solve equations using the distributive property.

Practice this skill →

The idea

The multiplier outside parentheses applies to every term inside: a(x + b) = ax + ab.
  1. Distribute the outside factor to both terms.
  2. Undo the constant term.
  3. Divide by the coefficient of x.

Example 1   2(x + 3) = 18

  1. Distribute: 2x + 6 = 18.
  2. Subtract 6: 2x = 12.
  3. Divide by 2: x = 6.

Example 2   3(x + 4) = 27

  1. Distribute: 3x + 12 = 27.
  2. Subtract 12: 3x = 15.
  3. Divide by 3: x = 5.
Watch for this

2(x + 3) is 2x + 6, not 2x + 3. Both terms inside the parentheses must be multiplied.

Area of a triangle

Find triangle area from perpendicular base and height.

Practice this skill →

The idea

A triangle has half the area of a rectangle with the same base and perpendicular height: A = ½ × b × h.
  1. Identify the base and its perpendicular height.
  2. Multiply base by height.
  3. Divide by 2 and use square units.

Example 1   Base = 8 cm, height = 5 cm. Find the area.

  1. 8 × 5 = 40.
  2. 40 ÷ 2 = 20 cm².

Example 2   Base = 12 m, height = 7 m. Find the area.

  1. 12 × 7 = 84.
  2. 84 ÷ 2 = 42 m².
Watch for this

The slanted side is not necessarily the height. Height must meet the base at a right angle.

Missing triangle angles

Use the angle sum of a triangle to find an unknown angle.

Practice this skill →

The idea

The three interior angles of a triangle add to 180°. Subtract the two known angles from 180°.
  1. Add the two known angles.
  2. Subtract their sum from 180°.
  3. Check all three angles now add to 180°.

Example 1   Two angles are 45° and 65°. Find the third.

  1. 45 + 65 = 110.
  2. 180 − 110 = 70°.

Example 2   A right triangle has another angle of 35°. Find the remaining angle.

  1. A right angle is 90°.
  2. 180 − 90 − 35 = 55°.
Watch for this

Use 180° for the interior angle sum of a triangle, not 360°.

The Pythagorean theorem

Find the hypotenuse of a right triangle.

Practice this skill →

The idea

For a right triangle, a² + b² = c². The hypotenuse c is opposite the right angle and is the longest side.
  1. Square each of the two leg lengths.
  2. Add those squares.
  3. Take the square root to find the hypotenuse.

Example 1   The legs are 3 and 4. Find the hypotenuse.

  1. 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25.
  2. c² = 25, so c = 5.

Example 2   The legs are 5 and 12. Find the hypotenuse.

  1. 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169.
  2. c = √169 = 13.
Watch for this

Adding the leg lengths does not find the hypotenuse. Square first, add, then take the square root.

Add signed integers

Add positive and negative integers.

Practice this skill →

The idea

Adding a positive number moves right on the number line. Adding a negative number moves left.
  1. Start at the first integer.
  2. Move in the direction of the second integer.
  3. Read the final position.

Example 1   −4 + 9

  1. Start at −4 and move 9 units right.
  2. The result is 5.

Example 2   3 + (−8)

  1. Start at 3 and move 8 units left.
  2. The result is −5.
Watch for this

The rule “two negatives make a positive” is for multiplication or division, not for adding two negative numbers.

Distance on a coordinate grid

Find horizontal distance between points.

Practice this skill →

The idea

When two points have the same y-coordinate, their distance is the absolute difference of their x-coordinates.
  1. Confirm the y-coordinates match.
  2. Subtract the smaller x-coordinate from the larger.
  3. Report the distance as a nonnegative number.

Example 1   Distance between (−2, 3) and (5, 3)

  1. Both points have y = 3.
  2. 5 − (−2) = 7 units.

Example 2   Distance between (−6, 1) and (−1, 1)

  1. Both points have y = 1.
  2. −1 − (−6) = 5 units.
Watch for this

Distance measures separation, so it cannot be negative.

Slope from two points

Calculate a line’s rate of change.

Practice this skill →

The idea

Slope is rise divided by run: m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). Keep the same point order in both differences.
  1. Find the change in y.
  2. Find the change in x using the same order.
  3. Divide rise by run.

Example 1   Slope through (1, 2) and (4, 8)

  1. Rise = 8 − 2 = 6. Run = 4 − 1 = 3.
  2. m = 6/3 = 2.

Example 2   Slope through (0, 5) and (2, 1)

  1. Rise = 1 − 5 = −4. Run = 2 − 0 = 2.
  2. m = −4/2 = −2.
Watch for this

A vertical line has zero run and undefined slope. It does not have slope zero.

Evaluate linear functions

Find outputs of a linear function.

Practice this skill →

The idea

For f(x) = mx + b, replace x with the given input. Multiply first, then add the constant.
  1. Substitute the input for x.
  2. Perform multiplication.
  3. Add or subtract the constant.

Example 1   f(x) = 3x + 2. Find f(4).

  1. f(4) = 3(4) + 2.
  2. f(4) = 14.

Example 2   g(x) = −2x + 5. Find g(−3).

  1. g(−3) = −2(−3) + 5.
  2. g(−3) = 11.
Watch for this

f(4) means the function’s output at input 4. It does not mean f multiplied by 4.

Systems by substitution

Find the intersection of two lines.

Practice this skill →

The idea

At an intersection, both equations have the same x and y values. If both equations give y, set their expressions equal.
  1. Set the expressions for y equal.
  2. Solve the resulting equation for x.
  3. Substitute x into either equation to find y and check both.

Example 1   y = 2x + 1 and y = x + 4

  1. 2x + 1 = x + 4, so x = 3.
  2. y = 2(3) + 1 = 7.
  3. The solution is (3, 7).

Example 2   y = 3x − 2 and y = x + 6

  1. 3x − 2 = x + 6, so 2x = 8 and x = 4.
  2. y = 10. The solution is (4, 10).
Watch for this

An x-value alone is not the full solution pair. These practice questions ask specifically for x; use y to check your work.

Quadratic equations by factoring

Find roots using the zero-product property.

Practice this skill →

The idea

If (x − a)(x − b) = 0, at least one factor is zero. The roots are x = a and x = b.
  1. Factor the quadratic into two binomials.
  2. Set each factor equal to zero.
  3. Solve both equations and check which root the question requests.

Example 1   x² − 7x + 12 = 0

  1. Factor: (x − 3)(x − 4) = 0.
  2. The roots are 3 and 4. The smaller root is 3.

Example 2   x² − 2x − 15 = 0

  1. Factor: (x − 5)(x + 3) = 0.
  2. The roots are 5 and −3. The smaller root is −3.
Watch for this

A quadratic may have two roots. Give the requested root rather than dropping one without checking.

The distance formula

Find distance between any two points.

Practice this skill →

The idea

Distance = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²). The differences are the legs of a right triangle.
  1. Find the horizontal and vertical changes.
  2. Square each change and add.
  3. Take the nonnegative square root.

Example 1   Distance from (1, 2) to (4, 6)

  1. Changes: 4 − 1 = 3 and 6 − 2 = 4.
  2. Distance = √(9 + 16) = 5.

Example 2   Distance from (−2, 1) to (3, 13)

  1. Changes: 5 horizontally and 12 vertically.
  2. Distance = √(25 + 144) = 13.
Watch for this

Square each difference before adding. Do not square the sum of the differences.

Exponent rules

Combine powers with the same base.

Practice this skill →

The idea

For the same nonzero base, multiply powers by adding exponents: a^m × a^n = a^(m+n). Divide powers by subtracting exponents.
  1. Check that the bases match.
  2. Add exponents for multiplication or subtract for division.
  3. Keep the original base.

Example 1   2³ × 2⁴ = 2^k. Find k.

  1. The bases match.
  2. k = 3 + 4 = 7.

Example 2   5⁶ ÷ 5² = 5^k. Find k.

  1. Subtract the exponents.
  2. k = 6 − 2 = 4.
Watch for this

Do not add the bases or multiply the exponents when multiplying powers with the same base.

Evaluate logarithms

Rewrite logarithms as exponential equations.

Practice this skill →

The idea

log_b(a) = c means b^c = a, where b > 0, b ≠ 1, and a > 0.
  1. Identify the base and argument.
  2. Ask what power of the base equals the argument.
  3. Check by exponentiation.

Example 1   log₂(32)

  1. 2⁵ = 32.
  2. Therefore log₂(32) = 5.

Example 2   log₃(1/9)

  1. 3^(−2) = 1/9.
  2. Therefore log₃(1/9) = −2.
Watch for this

The argument is not multiplied by the base. A logarithm asks for an exponent.

Right-triangle trigonometry

Find sine and cosine ratios in a right triangle.

Practice this skill →

The idea

For an acute angle θ, sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse and cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse.
  1. Locate the reference angle.
  2. Identify the opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse sides.
  3. Select the two sides used by the requested ratio.

Example 1   Opposite = 3, adjacent = 4, hypotenuse = 5. Find sin θ.

  1. Sine uses opposite over hypotenuse.
  2. sin θ = 3/5.

Example 2   Opposite = 5, adjacent = 12, hypotenuse = 13. Find cos θ.

  1. Cosine uses adjacent over hypotenuse.
  2. cos θ = 12/13.
Watch for this

Opposite and adjacent depend on the reference angle. The hypotenuse is always opposite the right angle.

Arithmetic sequences

Find a term of a sequence with constant difference.

Practice this skill →

The idea

The nth term is a_n = a₁ + (n − 1)d, where a₁ is the first term and d is the common difference.
  1. Identify the first term and common difference.
  2. Count n − 1 changes from the first term.
  3. Multiply the difference by n − 1 and add to the first term.

Example 1   4, 7, 10, … Find the 8th term.

  1. a₁ = 4 and d = 3.
  2. a₈ = 4 + 7(3) = 25.

Example 2   20, 16, 12, … Find the 6th term.

  1. a₁ = 20 and d = −4.
  2. a₆ = 20 + 5(−4) = 0.
Watch for this

The first term is already counted. Use n − 1 differences, not n differences.

The remainder theorem

Find the remainder when dividing by x − a.

Practice this skill →

The idea

When a polynomial f(x) is divided by x − a, the remainder is f(a).
  1. Identify a from the divisor x − a.
  2. Substitute a into the polynomial.
  3. Evaluate; the result is the remainder.

Example 1   Remainder of (x² + 3x + 2) ÷ (x − 2)

  1. Evaluate f(2).
  2. 2² + 3(2) + 2 = 12.

Example 2   Remainder of (x² − x + 4) ÷ (x + 1)

  1. x + 1 = x − (−1), so evaluate f(−1).
  2. (−1)² − (−1) + 4 = 6.
Watch for this

For a divisor x + 1, substitute −1, not 1.

Composition of functions

Evaluate one function inside another.

Practice this skill →

The idea

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). Evaluate the inside function first, then use its output as the input to the outside function.
  1. Find g(x) for the given input.
  2. Substitute that result into f.
  3. Evaluate f to finish.

Example 1   f(x) = 2x + 1, g(x) = x². Find f(g(3)).

  1. g(3) = 9.
  2. f(9) = 2(9) + 1 = 19.

Example 2   f(x) = x², g(x) = x + 4. Find f(g(2)).

  1. g(2) = 6.
  2. f(6) = 36.
Watch for this

f(g(x)) and g(f(x)) can have different values. Follow the requested order.

Geometric sequences

Find terms in a sequence with constant ratio.

Practice this skill →

The idea

The nth term is a_n = a₁ × r^(n−1), where r is the common ratio.
  1. Identify the first term and ratio.
  2. Raise the ratio to n − 1.
  3. Multiply by the first term.

Example 1   3, 6, 12, … Find the 6th term.

  1. a₁ = 3 and r = 2.
  2. a₆ = 3 × 2⁵ = 96.

Example 2   80, 40, 20, … Find the 5th term.

  1. a₁ = 80 and r = 1/2.
  2. a₅ = 80 × (1/2)⁴ = 5.
Watch for this

A constant ratio uses multiplication, unlike the constant difference of an arithmetic sequence.

Introduction to derivatives

Use the power rule to find a slope at a point.

Practice this skill →

The idea

For f(x) = ax^n, the derivative is f′(x) = an x^(n−1). A derivative gives the instantaneous rate of change. This is an introductory calculus topic.
  1. Multiply the coefficient by the exponent.
  2. Reduce the exponent by 1.
  3. Evaluate the derivative at the requested x-value.

Example 1   f(x) = 3x². Find f′(2).

  1. f′(x) = 6x.
  2. f′(2) = 12.

Example 2   f(x) = 2x³. Find f′(−1).

  1. f′(x) = 6x².
  2. f′(−1) = 6.
Watch for this

Evaluate the derivative, not the original function, when asked for the instantaneous rate of change.
